What Route 66's History Means for Glendora's Aging Plumbing

By the Glendora Pro Plumbing team

Historic Route 66 runs directly through Glendora, and the corridor along it, overlapping with parts of Foothill Boulevard and the original alignment through downtown, carries some of the city's oldest residential and commercial construction. That history is part of what gives this part of Glendora its character. It also means a lot of the plumbing underneath those properties is old enough to need real attention.

A Brief History of the Corridor Itself

Glendora's stretch of Route 66 followed a couple of different alignments over the decades as the road evolved from its original path through downtown to a later route further north, avoiding sharp turns and a railroad grade crossing.

Properties built along either alignment during the highway's active decades reflect the residential and commercial construction standards of that specific era. For plumbing purposes, that generally means galvanized supply lines and clay or early cast-iron sewer laterals, regardless of which exact stretch of the historic road a property sits on.

For a homeowner, that construction era is the single fact a technician needs before quoting a repair, since it determines the likely pipe material before anyone opens a wall.

What 'Old' Actually Means for a Pipe

A home along this corridor typically dates to a period when galvanized steel was the default choice for residential supply piping, paired with clay sewer laterals depending on the era. Neither material was a mistake at the time, both were standard, reasonable choices for residential construction for decades. The issue is simply time: galvanized steel corrodes from the inside over many decades regardless of how well it was installed, and clay sewer pipe develops cracks and joint separations as the ground shifts around it year after year.

Glendora's own alluvial soil, which expands and contracts with the seasons, adds ongoing stress to both pipe types. A property along this corridor has had many decades of that seasonal cycle working on its original infrastructure, which is a meaningfully different situation than a home built in the 1990s or later.

The Two Failure Patterns We See Most

On the supply side, original galvanized lines show up as declining water pressure, rusty water, and eventually a burst pipe with no obvious external warning. On the sewer side, older clay laterals combined with decades of mature landscaping along this historic corridor are a near-perfect setup for root intrusion, which shows up as recurring backups that a simple snake only temporarily resolves.

What a Camera Inspection Actually Reveals

A sewer camera run through an original clay lateral on a Route 66-era property often tells a clearer story than the pipe's age alone would suggest. Some lines from this period, if the soil above them has been relatively undisturbed and free of aggressive root systems, are still in reasonable condition. Others, particularly near mature landscaping or under areas that have seen repeated soil movement, show multiple problem points that only become visible once a camera is actually run through the line.

That's really the point of checking rather than assuming: a home's age tells you what to expect, but only a direct inspection tells you what's actually happening underneath a specific property.

What This Means If You're Buying or Renovating

If you're purchasing a home along Glendora's Route 66 corridor, a plumbing inspection focused specifically on supply line material and sewer lateral condition is worth requesting beyond a standard general inspection, general home inspectors don't always catch galvanized pipe or run a sewer camera as standard practice. If you already own a home here and are planning a renovation, that's also a natural point to address a repiping project before walls are opened for other work.

What to Ask Before You Buy an Older Property Here

If you're specifically evaluating a property along this corridor, asking directly about known plumbing history, any past repipe work, sewer line repairs, or documented issues, gives you a starting point beyond what a general inspection covers. Sellers aren't always aware of the full plumbing history themselves, especially in a home that's changed hands multiple times, which is exactly why an independent camera inspection and pipe material check matter regardless of what you're told.

It's Not Unique to Glendora

This same history plays out along other stretches of the historic Route 66 alignment through the San Gabriel Valley, including through Covina's own historic downtown core, incorporated in 1901 and carrying a similar mix of original-era construction alongside decades of later infill.

FAQs

Common Questions

Does a home's location on Route 66 itself matter, or just its age?

It's really about age and construction era rather than proximity to the road itself. Properties built during the same period elsewhere in Glendora face the same considerations.

Are commercial properties along this corridor affected the same way?

Yes, older commercial buildings along the corridor face the same galvanized supply and clay sewer lateral risk as residential properties from the same era, often with heavier daily water demand adding extra strain.

Do standard home inspections check for galvanized pipe or sewer line condition?

Not always thoroughly. A general home inspector may note visible pipe material but typically doesn't run a sewer camera. This is why we recommend a dedicated plumbing inspection for older properties before you buy.

Is it worth repiping a home along this corridor even without an active problem?

It depends on the pipe's actual condition and your renovation timeline. If you're already planning to open walls for other work, addressing supply piping at the same time is usually more cost-effective than doing it as a separate emergency project later, once a leak has already caused damage. A licensed contractor familiar with the era can usually spot the likely pipe material within minutes of arriving.
