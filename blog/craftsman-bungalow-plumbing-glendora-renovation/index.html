Craftsman Bungalow Plumbing: What to Know Before You Renovate in Glendora

By the Glendora Pro Plumbing team

Craftsman bungalows near Glendora Village are some of the most character-rich homes in the city, and renovating one is a genuinely rewarding project. They're also old enough that the plumbing behind the walls almost certainly predates several generations of code updates, material improvements, and household water-use expectations, which makes a plumbing assessment worth doing before, not during, your remodel, when changes are far cheaper and less disruptive to make.

Balancing Preservation With Practical Updates

Many Craftsman renovations aim to preserve the home's original character, original trim, built-ins, window placement, while modernizing what's actually functional. Plumbing fits naturally into that second category: there's no historical or aesthetic reason to keep original galvanized supply lines or an outdated valve, since none of it is visible once walls are closed back up. Preservation-minded homeowners can update the plumbing fully without compromising the architectural features that make the home worth renovating in the first place.

What's Likely Already There

A Craftsman built in the early-to-mid 20th century most likely started with galvanized steel supply piping, and depending on the specific era, may have clay or early cast-iron drain and sewer lines. Decades of prior renovations may have replaced some sections with copper without fully updating the system, which means it's common to find a genuine patchwork of different pipe materials and ages within the same house rather than one consistent system.

Why This Matters for a Kitchen or Bathroom Remodel

Opening a wall for a kitchen or bathroom renovation is the single most cost-effective time to address aging supply lines, since the labor to access the plumbing is already part of the project rather than a separate expense later. A shower or tub installation in a Craftsman bathroom, in particular, is a natural point to also address an outdated, non-pressure-balanced valve, a common issue in homes this age that causes water temperature to swing when another fixture runs elsewhere in the house.

If your remodel plans don't currently include a plumbing assessment, it's worth adding one before finishes go in, rather than discovering a galvanized supply line needs attention after the new tile is already down.

Permits and What Inspectors Look For

Significant plumbing changes during a renovation, including repiping and new fixture rough-ins, typically require a permit and inspection under local building code. An inspector will specifically check that abandoned old piping is properly capped rather than left in place, that new work meets current code even where it connects to older existing lines, and that any water heater or gas-related changes meet current venting requirements. Building these steps into your project timeline from the start avoids delays discovered mid-renovation.

The Sewer Line Question

Beyond the visible remodel area, it's worth having your main sewer line camera-inspected if it hasn't been done recently, especially given how often mature landscaping around these older properties has led to root intrusion in original clay laterals. Finding this out during planning gives you options; finding it out after your remodel is finished means dealing with a sewer line repair that could disrupt work you've just completed.

Coordinating With Your Contractor or Designer

If you're working with a general contractor or interior designer on your Craftsman renovation, looping in a plumber early, ideally during the design phase rather than once demolition has started, lets everyone plan around the plumbing rather than discovering a conflict mid-project. This is especially true for layout changes that move a kitchen or bathroom fixture to a new location, since that almost always means new drain and vent routing through walls or floors that weren't originally built to accommodate it.

A Practical Starting Point

Before finalizing your renovation plans, a plumber can walk through the home, identify visible pipe materials in accessible areas like a basement, crawlspace, or garage, and flag anything worth a closer look before you commit to a specific scope. It's a modest upfront step that avoids the far more disruptive scenario of a mid-renovation surprise.

The same guidance applies to Craftsman and early-1900s housing stock in Azusa's own older neighborhoods near downtown, which share a nearly identical construction era and material profile, right down to the same likely mix of galvanized supply lines and clay sewer laterals waiting to be found once someone actually looks.

FAQs

Common Questions

Do I have to repipe my whole Craftsman during a remodel?

Not necessarily, though if the assessment reveals significant galvanized pipe throughout, addressing the whole system at once while walls are already open is often more cost-effective than a partial fix now and a bigger project later.

Can I keep my original clawfoot tub while updating the plumbing behind it?

In most cases yes, since updating supply and drain connections doesn't require replacing the fixture itself unless the tub has its own structural or leak issues.

Will a plumbing assessment slow down my renovation timeline?

A basic assessment is typically quick, often completed in a single visit, and it's far less disruptive than discovering a plumbing issue mid-project after finishes are already installed.

Is it normal for an old house to have mixed pipe materials?

Yes, it's common in homes that have had partial renovations over the decades. A plumber can identify what's actually present and recommend whether a full or partial repipe makes sense given your specific renovation goals. A contractor experienced with this housing era can often spot other likely issues in the same walk-through.
