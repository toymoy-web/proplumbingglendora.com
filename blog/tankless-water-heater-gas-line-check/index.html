Why Your New Tankless Water Heater Needs a Gas Line Check First

By the Glendora Pro Plumbing team

A tankless water heater genuinely demands significantly more gas flow, essentially all at once, than the tank unit it's actually replacing. That's an easy detail to overlook when you're focused on the unit itself, but it's often the single biggest factor in whether an installation goes smoothly or turns into a bigger project than expected.

A Real Example of How This Plays Out

A common scenario looks like this: a homeowner researches tankless units, picks a model based on reviews and specifications, purchases it, and schedules an installation, only to have the installer discover on-site that the existing half-inch gas line serving a decades-old tank unit can't deliver the BTU load the new tankless model requires. At that point, either the project pauses for a gas line upgrade, or a smaller, less capable tankless unit gets installed as a compromise. Both outcomes are avoidable with an assessment done before the purchase, not after.

Why Tankless Units Need More Gas

A tank water heater heats water gradually and stores it. This means its burner only needs to produce a moderate, steady amount of heat over time. A tankless unit heats water instantly, on demand, as it flows through. This means the burner needs to deliver a substantially larger burst of heat output the instant someone turns on a hot tap. That translates directly into a higher gas flow requirement, measured in BTUs, than most existing tank installations were ever sized for.

Why This Is Especially Relevant in Glendora

Homes dating to Glendora's earliest development, particularly the Craftsman-era properties near the Village, were often built with gas lines sized for a much smaller overall household gas load than a modern home might have: a single water heater, a stove, maybe a furnace, with reasonable margin for the era but not for adding a high-demand appliance decades later.

We regularly find this exact situation when a homeowner in this part of the city decides to switch to tankless: the existing line simply can't support the new unit's gas demand without an upgrade.

How Much Line Capacity a Tankless Unit Actually Needs

A typical whole-home tankless water heater requires significantly more BTU capacity than a standard 40-gallon tank unit, and that requirement compounds if the same gas line also feeds a furnace, range, or fireplace that might run at the same time as the water heater. The assessment isn't just about the tankless unit in isolation, it's about total demand across every appliance sharing that line, which is easy to underestimate if you're only thinking about the one new appliance you're adding.

What Happens If You Skip This Check

A tankless unit connected to an undersized gas line won't necessarily fail outright, but it will underperform, delivering lower water temperature than expected, especially when another gas appliance runs at the same time, or tripping error codes related to insufficient gas pressure. That's a genuinely real and frustrating outcome after paying good money for a premium appliance. It's entirely avoidable with a proper assessment before installation rather than after, which is a standard part of how we quote every tankless project from the start, rather than something we mention only after a problem has already shown up during installation.

Sizing the New Line Correctly

When an upgrade is needed, sizing it only for today's tankless unit is a missed opportunity if you're planning any other gas appliance additions down the road, an outdoor kitchen, a fireplace insert, a pool heater. It's worth discussing your broader plans during the assessment rather than sizing strictly for the immediate project, since running a larger line once is more cost-effective than upgrading again in a few years for the next addition.

What a Proper Check Actually Involves

A gas line assessment confirms your existing line's size, carefully calculates the total real BTU demand across every gas appliance in your home including the new tankless unit, and identifies whether an upgrade or extension is needed. If it is, that becomes part of the same project as your water heater installation rather than a surprise discovered after the new unit is already connected and underperforming.

This exact check-first approach matters just as much in San Dimas, where similar-era housing near the original town center faces the same undersized-line risk when switching to tankless.

FAQs

Common Questions

How do I know if my gas line can support a tankless water heater?

A plumber can calculate your home's total gas demand, including the new unit, against your existing line's actual capacity. This is worth doing before purchasing the unit, not after.

Does every tankless installation need a gas line upgrade?

No, some homes already have adequate capacity, particularly newer construction. The only way to know for certain is a proper assessment rather than assuming either way.

How much does a gas line upgrade typically add to a tankless installation?

It depends on how much of the line needs upgrading and how accessible it is, but it's worth budgeting for as a possibility rather than assuming your existing line is automatically sufficient.

Can I still get a tank water heater if my gas line isn't sized for tankless?

Yes, a tank replacement generally doesn't require the same gas line capacity as tankless, so it remains a straightforward option if a gas line upgrade doesn't make sense for your budget or timeline right now. It's a short conversation that can save a considerable amount of frustration after the unit is already installed.
