Why Ficus Trees Are Clogging Sewer Lines Near Glendora Village

By the Glendora Pro Plumbing team

The ficus trees lining Glendora Avenue through downtown Glendora Village are part of what makes the area feel the way it does, mature, shaded, established. They're also, quietly, one of the leading causes of recurring sewer line backups in the homes and businesses nearby. It's worth understanding why before you assume a repeat clog is just bad luck.

Why Ficus Roots Specifically Are a Problem

Ficus trees have an aggressive, shallow, fast-spreading root system that actively seeks out moisture. A sewer line carrying wastewater is exactly the kind of steady underground moisture source those roots are drawn to.

They don't need much of an opening to get in either, a hairline crack or a slightly separated joint in an older clay or cast-iron lateral is enough. Once a root finds its way in, it doesn't stay small. Over years it thickens into a dense mass inside the pipe that traps paper, grease, and debris, which is what actually causes the repeat backups.

This isn't unique to Glendora, it's a documented pattern across Southern California wherever ficus trees were planted as street or residential landscaping decades ago. It's specifically why some cities no longer plant ficus as a street tree at all. Glendora's own ficus-lined streets, prized for the shade and character they bring to the Village, carry the same underlying risk to the older clay and cast-iron sewer laterals running underneath.

Why a Simple Snake Doesn't Fix It

A cable snake clears a clog by cutting a channel through whatever's blocking the line, root mass included. That's why a snaked line often clogs again within weeks or months, the root mass itself is still there, just with a new hole punched through the middle of it, and it regrows into that space quickly. Hydro jetting works differently: high-pressure water scours the entire interior wall of the pipe, actually clearing the root material rather than just opening a path through it. That's the real reason it keeps a line clear much longer against this specific kind of recurring problem.

Why Some Cities Have Stopped Planting Ficus

Because the root-intrusion pattern is so well documented, a number of Southern California cities no longer plant ficus as a street tree at all, specifically citing sidewalk and underground utility damage. Glendora's own established ficus canopy predates that shift. This means the trees along the Village's streets are old enough that their root systems have had decades to reach whatever sewer infrastructure sits nearby, regardless of how well the original pipe was installed.

Signs the Problem Is Root Intrusion, Not Something Else

A main line that backs up repeatedly despite being cleared, gurgling from multiple drains when one fixture runs, and a sewer smell that comes and goes rather than staying constant are all consistent with root intrusion specifically. A camera inspection is the only way to actually confirm it, and it also tells us whether the line has developed a crack or separated joint that will need sewer line repair in addition to clearing out the roots that got in through it.

What Happens During a Camera Inspection

A sewer camera is fed directly into the line, typically through an existing cleanout or a fixture like a toilet, and transmits live video back to a monitor so we can see the actual interior condition of the pipe in real time. Root intrusion is usually unmistakable on camera, visible root masses growing into the pipe interior, sometimes extending several feet along the line from a single entry point. This also lets us measure roughly how far into the line the roots have traveled, which matters for choosing between a localized repair and a longer trenchless lining.

What Actually Helps Long-Term

Removing a mature, established ficus isn't usually a realistic answer, and we don't recommend it as a first step. A confirmed repair or trenchless lining that seals the opening the roots were using stops new intrusion at that specific point, even with the tree still standing nearby. For a line with root intrusion at multiple points along its length, that's often the point where a fuller repair makes more sense than repeated spot fixes.

This same pattern shows up just as often in Covina's older downtown neighborhoods, where decades of mature landscaping sit above the same era of original clay sewer laterals.

FAQs

Common Questions

Do I need to remove my ficus tree if it's causing sewer problems?

Not necessarily. A confirmed repair or trenchless lining that seals the entry point often resolves the recurring backups without removing an established tree, though removal is sometimes the more practical answer for a severely compromised line.

Are ficus trees the only species that cause this problem?

No, though they're especially aggressive. Other trees with fast, shallow root systems can cause similar intrusion given enough time and a nearby moisture source like a sewer line.

How do I know if my recurring clog is from tree roots?

A camera inspection is the only reliable way to confirm it. Recurring backups in the same line despite regular clearing, especially near mature landscaping, are a strong indicator worth checking.

Is hydro jetting safe for an older clay sewer line?

We assess the line's condition on camera first. For a severely compromised older line, a gentler approach or a repair may be the safer call than full-pressure jetting, since the goal is clearing the blockage without further damaging an already-weakened pipe. An arborist can also advise on root management options if the tree itself is a long-term concern for you.
